## Slide 1 — Introduction (0:00–0:55)

“Good morning. We are Team Genesis, and our project is CliffForge. We are working on a research problem in early drug discovery: when a chemist changes a promising molecule slightly, can we predict whether that change will improve its activity, and can we recognize a surprising change called an activity cliff?

I want to set expectations clearly. This is actual research work. A complete system would need reliable protein-level modeling and experimental validation; that cannot be built in a day. What we can show today is a computational proof of concept: a pipeline that proposes close analogues, predicts their relative activity, and produces a ranked shortlist. The molecule drawing on the right illustrates a small edit that corresponds to a large potency difference.”

“Before getting into our method, here is a short protein–ligand binding simulation to give physical context. A ligand interacts with a protein binding pocket in three dimensions, so even a small chemical edit can change those interactions. This visual illustrates the research problem. It is not a simulation generated by CliffForge; our current proof of concept uses two-dimensional molecular data and has not performed docking.”

## Slide 2 — Problem statement and validation (1:30–2:40)

“An activity cliff is a pair of chemically similar molecules with a large measured potency difference. In our study, we define similarity using a molecular fingerprint score of at least 0.80, and call a difference of one pIC50 unit a cliff. That is roughly a tenfold potency change. This matters when a chemist must choose which analogue to make next: similarity by itself does not tell us which small edit is valuable.

We study the open COVID Moonshot dataset for the SARS-CoV-2 main protease, or Mpro. Among 1,102 molecules, the full dataset contains 46 cliff pairs, but only five have both molecules held out from training. That makes cliff detection a difficult, small-sample test. The chart gives the other motivation: a standard ChemProp model has strong global ranking, around 0.78 Spearman correlation, but its average ranking *within* related chemical series is only about 0.19. Our research question is whether learning differences between related molecules can improve that local decision.”

## Slide 3 — Proposed solution and innovation (2:40–3:45)

“CliffForge has three linked parts. First, it proposes close analogues by reusing substituent changes actually observed in the training data. These are traceable, single-edit suggestions, not molecules invented by a free-form generator.

Second, instead of predicting each compound’s potency in isolation, our Siamese neural network compares two molecules and predicts the *difference* in pIC50 between a candidate and a starting molecule. That matches the decision a medicinal chemist makes: is this edit likely to help relative to what I already have?

Third, we combine predictions from three independently trained models. Their spread gives a practical uncertainty signal, which enters the ranking and accompanies potential activity-cliff alerts. The solid boxes are implemented in our computational pipeline. The dashed protein-pocket scoring box is our planned next stage.”

## Slide 4 — Technical methodology (3:45–4:50)

“Here is the pipeline in order. We start with a seed ligand represented as a SMILES string and a training set of 961 compounds with measured Mpro activity. We find sites that can be edited and mine matched molecular pair transformations from the training compounds. A transformation needs at least two precedents in our reference settings.

We apply those changes to the seed, then use RDKit to reject invalid or duplicate structures and to enforce similarity, edit-size, and medicinal-chemistry filters. For each surviving candidate, the three-model ensemble predicts candidate-minus-seed delta pIC50. We rank by predicted gain minus the ensemble standard deviation, and report the transformation, score, uncertainty, and any potential-cliff alert.

This output is a set of hypotheses for a scientist to review. Protein docking, three-dimensional effects, synthesis, and laboratory testing come later.”

## Slide 5 — Prototype and proof of concept (4:50–6:25)

“The first panel is our strongest result on the main Moonshot benchmark. Across 12 held-out chemical series, mean within-series Spearman ranking correlation is 0.581 for the relative model versus 0.192 for the ChemProp absolute-activity baseline. The paired test gives a p-value of 0.005. The small FEP-plus line refers to earlier internship research that motivated this project; it is not a new CliffForge experiment.

The middle panel shows what the prototype actually does for one held-out seed. It attempted 324 training-derived edits, produced 231 unique candidates, and retained and scored 34 after filtering. These are model-ranked structures. Even the top candidate has only a modest *predicted* gain, and none of these generated molecules has been synthesized, assayed, or docked.

The final panel is a preliminary cliff result: among 44 close pairs with both molecules held out, there are five true cliffs. CliffForge flagged two; ChemProp flagged none at the selected thresholds. Five positives are far too few for a firm superiority claim. The retrospective generation check also recovered 82 of 316 reachable held-out analogue pairs; the model got the direction right for 53 of those 82, versus 47 for ChemProp. That difference was not statistically significant.”

## Slide 6 — Larger, separate retrospective evaluation (6:25–8:20)

“This slide tests a related idea on a larger Dablander matched-pair dataset of 1,924 Mpro molecules. I want to keep it separate from the previous experiment: it uses a different dataset and a different comparison protocol.

For held-out test molecules compared with training references, there are 25 cliff pairs among 223 close pairs. In this notebook evaluation, CliffForge flagged 13 of the 25 at the stated threshold. The two baselines flagged none, and the average-precision chart, which rewards ranking true cliffs highly, also favors the relative model. On 63 test–test pairs with a cliff-sized activity difference, it predicted the direction correctly 96.8 percent of the time, compared with 81 percent for ChemProp. Those are promising retrospective signals.

The third panel adds an important boundary: average within-series ranking is nearly tied here, 0.311 versus 0.304. Also, this notebook’s ChemProp comparison constructs a difference from two predicted activities, whereas the main lead-optimization evaluation can use the measured training reference. So I would treat this slide as a reason to run stronger, harmonized validation, not as proof that the method already wins in real lead optimization. Both datasets concern Mpro; testing other targets is still ahead.”

## Slide 7 — Impact, business model, and next steps (8:20–10:00)

“If further validation holds up, the practical value is to help medicinal chemists focus synthesis and assay effort on a smaller, better-explained set of analogues. Our prospective users would be academic drug-discovery teams, biotech groups, and pharmaceutical research teams. These are intended users, not confirmed partners.

The next research steps are to test many more held-out cliff pairs, add protein-structure and docking information, account for stereochemistry and three-dimensional effects, expand the transformation library, and eventually test selected candidates in the lab. Those experiments would tell us whether the model’s predictions translate into useful chemistry.

There is also a possible business route. CHAI Discovery is an AI molecular-design company, especially known for protein and antibody design. It offers commercial access to its models and platform and works with pharmaceutical companies through licenses and research collaborations. For example, Pfizer has licensed access to its platform and a custom model. A future CliffForge business model could similarly license a lead-optimization workflow to research organizations or build it with pharmaceutical partners. That is a proposal, not a tested commercial plan. CHAI’s product is much more mature and addresses a different molecular-design setting than our present small-molecule proof of concept.

Our message today is that relative-activity learning can make local analogue ranking more useful. The next step is to validate that signal more broadly and experimentally. Thank you.”
